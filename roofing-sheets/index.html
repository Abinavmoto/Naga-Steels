The thickness question, and the trap in it

Thickness is where most roofing sheet quotes go wrong, and it is entirely deliberate on the part of some sellers. A sheet can be quoted as TCT (total coated thickness, including the zinc and the paint) or as BMT (base metal thickness, the steel alone).

The difference is often 0.04–0.06 mm. So a sheet advertised as "0.50 mm" TCT may be only 0.45 mm of actual steel, while another seller quoting 0.45 mm BMT is offering you the identical product and looking more expensive for it.

Always ask which one a quote refers to. We quote TCT and say so. If you are comparing us against another rate, make sure you are comparing the same measurement.

The thickness you need is driven by purlin spacing, not by the roof area. A thin sheet over widely spaced purlins will oil-can, drum in the wind and eventually crack at the fixings. If you already know your purlin spacing, tell us and we will confirm the thickness.

Colour coated, galvalume, or plain GI?

Colour coated (PPGI)

Galvanized steel with a factory-applied paint system baked over a primer. Available in the usual range of blues, reds, greens and greys. The paint is not merely decorative: it is the primary corrosion barrier, and it also reflects a meaningful amount of solar heat, which is not a small consideration for a shed roof in Madurai in May. This is the most common choice for both sheds and residential roofing.

Galvalume (GL)

Coated with an aluminium-zinc alloy rather than pure zinc. It resists corrosion substantially better than plain GI, typically two to four times the life in the same conditions, and reflects heat well in its bare state. It is the right answer for coastal districts and for industrial atmospheres. Costs more than GI, less than a premium coated system.

Plain GI sheet

Uncoated, bright galvanized. Cheapest per square foot, runs hotter underneath, and is the standard for temporary structures, cattle shelters and anywhere appearance is irrelevant.

Rolling shutter sheet

Formed GI sections for shop and godown shutters, supplied in the standard widths and gauges. Tell us the shutter opening dimensions and we will work out the sections.

Cut to rafter length, and why it saves money

Standard-length sheets force you to overlap, and every overlap is material you paid for and cannot use. Cutting to your rafter length removes that waste entirely, and it also removes the horizontal lap joint, which is the single most common place a roof leaks after a few monsoons.

Measure from the ridge to the eave, add your required overhang, typically 6 to 9 inches, and give us that figure. We cut to it.

Working out the sheet count

Corrugated sheet has an overall width and an effective (covering) width, and they are not the same, the difference is the side lap. Always calculate on the effective width or you will come up short.

Number of sheets = roof width ÷ effective width, rounded up. Then add one spare sheet for cutting at hips, valleys and around any vent. For a simple gable roof, do this for each slope separately.

Send us the roof dimensions rather than a sheet count if you would prefer, we will do the arithmetic and tell you what it comes to before you commit.
